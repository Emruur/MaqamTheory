Chapter 01What Is a Makam?

A makam is not just a scale. It is a complete melodic personality: a set of pitches, yes, but also a home note, a second center of gravity, a characteristic direction of travel, and habits of phrasing that musicians have refined for centuries.

A scale plus a way of moving

If you come from Western music, your first instinct will be to ask “which notes does this makam use?” — the way you'd describe D minor or G major. That question gets you about a third of the way. A makam (makam, plural makamlar) is defined by three things working together:

ComponentTurkish termWhat it means
The pitch materialdizi (scale/row) Which notes are used, including microtonal inflections finer than the piano's twelve.
The functional notesdurak, güçlü, yeden The tonic where melodies come to rest, the “dominant” where they pause midway, and the leading tone that pulls back home.
The melodic journeyseyir (course, itinerary) Where melodies begin, how they rise and fall, which notes they linger on, and how they cadence. Two makams can share every pitch and still be different makams because their seyir differs.

Hear the difference yourself. First, the bare scale of makam Rast — just the ladder of notes. Then a short seyir sketch: the same notes, but moving the way Rast actually moves.

The second clip is still not a composition — it is a skeleton of typical behavior: start at the tonic, establish the lower region, rise to the fifth, touch the high tonic, and settle back down. That behavioral skeleton is what a Turkish musician means when they say “this melody is in Rast.”

Where makam music lives

The makam system is the melodic language of a huge family of music: Ottoman court and classical music (Türk sanat müziği), Anatolian folk song (türkü), Mevlevi and other Sufi ceremonial music, the call to prayer and Qur'anic recitation, urban fasıl entertainment music, and — with variations in tuning and terminology — the Arabic maqam and Persian dastgah traditions. When you learn Turkish makam theory you are learning one dialect, and arguably the most systematically documented one, of a musical language spoken from the Balkans to Central Asia.

Its characteristic instruments — the ney (reed flute), tanbur (long-necked lute), oud (ud), kanun (plucked zither), and kemençe (bowed fiddle) — are all capable of the fine pitch shadings the system requires. There is no piano in a classical makam ensemble, and this is not an accident: the piano literally cannot play most makams in tune.

Why the piano can't play it

Western music divides the octave into 12 equal semitones. Turkish theory divides it into 53 commas — a much finer grid. A Western whole tone equals 9 commas, and makam music routinely uses notes that sit 1, 4, 5, or 8 commas away from their neighbors. The most beloved notes of the repertoire — like the slightly-lowered third of Rast and Uşşak called segâh — fall between the piano's keys. Chapter 2 is entirely about this tuning system, with audio so you can actually hear a single comma.

Theory vs. practice Everything on this site follows the standard textbook system (Arel–Ezgi–Uzdilek, taught in Turkish conservatories since the mid-20th century). Real performers bend it: an Uşşak singer drops the second degree lower than the books say, and intonation shifts with emotion and register. Learn the grid first; then, when you listen to masters, enjoy how they lean away from it.

The five makams of this course

There are dozens of makams in active use and hundreds in the theory books. These five — the ones this course teaches — are among the most common, most characteristic, and most useful for a beginner:

MakamTonicOne-line personality
Rastrast (G)Bright, noble, foundational — often called the “mother of makams.”
Nihavendrast (G)The closest to a Western minor key; elegant, urban, romantic.
Uşşakdügâh (A)Warm and plaintive, with the famous low second degree; the sound of countless folk songs and hymns.
Hüseynidügâh (A)Open, spacious, heroic — the characteristic sound of Anatolian türkü.
Hicazdügâh (A)Instantly recognizable “oriental” color from its augmented second; passionate and ornate.

How the course is organized

Chapters 2–4 build your toolkit: the comma system, how to read Turkish sheet music, and the tetrachord/pentachord building blocks from which every makam is assembled. Chapters 5–9 then take one makam each, in an order chosen for your ear: Rast (the reference point), Nihavend (familiar territory), Uşşak and Hüseyni (the microtonal siblings), and Hicaz (the dramatic one). Chapter 10 puts them side by side and trains your ear to tell them apart.
